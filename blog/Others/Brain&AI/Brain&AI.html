What Can We Do in This AI Cycle?

There is a bigger question lurking beneath the daily headlines about AI progress: can humans and AI remain in a genuine cycle of mutual iteration — or are we approaching a point where AI simply outpaces and leaves human intelligence behind?

The Loop We've Come to Depend On

For years now, progress in AI has depended on a tight feedback loop between humans and machines. We shape AI, and AI's advances in turn reshape what we're capable of — a cycle that has, until recently, played to the strengths of both sides. AI has been able to advance in large part because of the gap between human and machine cognition: humans supply judgment, correction, and direction; AI supplies scale and speed. We guide the system so that it can iterate on itself.

What increasingly worries people is that this arrangement assumes we can still judge what AI produces — and that assumption is starting to break down. In the past, when we sat down to chat with a model or review AI-generated code, we could offer meaningful suggestions because we understood, at least roughly, what the output meant and why it worked or didn't. But what happens when we no longer can?

Today that loop runs through a few channels: humans set the loss functions and reward signals during training, feed in preferences and corrections that become training data in their own right (as in RLHF), and catch errors turn by turn in chat and code review. All of it depends on humans being able to understand AI's output well enough to correct it — and as models advance, that understanding is getting harder to sustain. The traditional method of judgment, where a person can simply inspect the output and tell whether it's good, is starting to fail.

A Widening Speed Gap

Underneath this is a simpler and more uncomfortable asymmetry: AI learns astonishingly fast, while humans learn astonishingly slowly. Our biological and cultural evolution moves on the scale of generations; a model's capabilities can shift within a single training run. If we hold our own development constant while AI's trajectory keeps climbing, the gap doesn't just persist — it compounds, and we fall further and further behind.

In Liu Cixin's novels, less advanced civilizations end up as little more than pets — or food — for those that have outpaced them technologically. It's a stark image, but it raises the real question of this era: in a world shaped by advanced AI, do we risk becoming the pets?

Staying at the Center

The answer this essay argues for is that humans should — and can — remain central, even as AI advances. But staying central isn't passive; it requires us to accelerate our own evolution rather than simply hoping the gap closes on its own.

That likely means going as far as editing our own biology — our genes — to make fuller use of the brains we already have. Natural evolution is far too slow to keep pace with AI on any relevant timescale; if we wait for selection pressure to reshape human cognition the old-fashioned way, AI will have moved on long before we do. Deliberate intervention is the only lever fast enough to matter.

There's also real reason to think our current cognitive architecture has room to improve. The efficiency of the brain's existing connectivity is genuinely questionable — the way information routes between regions, and the neurotransmitter systems that govern learning, attention, and memory, weren't optimized for the demands we're now placing on them.

A concrete path forward looks like this: first, build a digital model of the brain — a simulation detailed enough to capture connectivity patterns, signaling dynamics, and the parameters that govern them. Use that model to search, computationally, for network configurations and parameter sets that run more efficiently than what evolution happened to give us — the neural equivalent of architecture search for a model. Once we know what a more efficient brain would look like, the next step is mapping the relationship between genes and that model: which genes shape which connectivity patterns, which neurotransmitter systems, which parameters. That gene–model mapping is what would actually make targeted gene editing possible — not editing genes speculatively, but editing them toward a configuration we've already validated in simulation. In the nearer term, before that mapping is reliable enough to act on, invasive neural stimulation may offer an intermediate path — directly augmenting the brain's learning capacity rather than waiting on biology to catch up.

The core claim is simple: if we want to remain partners to AI rather than its dependents, we have to treat our own cognitive evolution as an engineering problem, not just a biological inevitability — and that means modeling the brain with the same rigor we now bring to modeling AI.
